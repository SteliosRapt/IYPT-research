Self-starting Siphon Priming: Geometry, Dynamics, and Loss

Models

Part I: Geometry and Lossless Equation of Motion

Pipe Geometry

We describe the pipe centerline by a sequence of points

\[ A(0, h_0),\quad B(x_1, h_1),\quad C(x_2, h_2),\quad D(x_3, h_3),\quad E(x_4, h_4), \]

where the horizontal coordinates are $x_i$ and the vertical coordinates are $h_i$. The lengths of the straight segments between consecutive points are

\begin{align} L_1 &= \lvert AB \rvert = \sqrt{x_1^2 + (h_1 - h_0)^2}, \\ L_2 &= \lvert BC \rvert = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (h_2 - h_1)^2}, \\ L_3 &= \lvert CD \rvert = \sqrt{(x_3 - x_2)^2 + (h_3 - h_2)^2}, \\ L_4 &= \lvert DE \rvert = \sqrt{(x_4 - x_3)^2 + (h_4 - h_3)^2}. \end{align}

The total pipe length is

\[ L_{\max} = L_1 + L_2 + L_3 + L_4. \]

Height Along the Pipe

Let $L$ denote the arc-length coordinate measured along the pipe from point $A$. We define a piecewise height function $h(L)$ along the pipe. For each straight segment we use a local parameter in $[0, 1]$; the functions $\tilde h_1, \tilde h_2, \tilde h_3, \tilde h_4$ map that local parameter to the physical height on each segment:

\[ h(L) = \begin{cases} \tilde h_1\!\left(\dfrac{L}{L_1}\right), & 0 \le L \le L_1, \\[1.0em] \tilde h_2\!\left(\dfrac{L - L_1}{L_2}\right), & L_1 \le L \le L_1 + L_2, \\[1.0em] \tilde h_3\!\left(\dfrac{L - L_1 - L_2}{L_3}\right), & L_1 + L_2 \le L \le L_1 + L_2 + L_3, \\[1.0em] \tilde h_4\!\left(\dfrac{L - L_1 - L_2 - L_3}{L_4}\right), & L_1 + L_2 + L_3 \le L \le L_{\max}. \end{cases} \]

If each segment is straight, then each $\tilde h_i$ is an affine function of its local parameter.

Container Water Level

We choose a reference time $t_0$ at which

  • the water plug in the pipe has length $L(t_0)$,
  • the container water height (free surface) is $h_c(t_0)$,
  • the pipe cross-sectional area is $A$,
  • the container free-surface area is $A_0$.

Let $V_c(t)$ be the volume of water in the container outside the pipe walls. During the stage when water is still entering the pipe from the container, the volumetric flow rate into the pipe is

\[ Q(t) = A\,\dot L_{\text{in}}(t), \]

where $\dot L_{\text{in}}$ is the rate at which the water front in the pipe moves away from the container. During the initial filling phase we will have $\dot L_{\text{in}} = \dot L(t)$; later, once the pipe is completely filled, we must treat $L$ as constant and use a different description (see Part II).

For the container alone, mass conservation gives

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}V_c}{\mathrm{d}t} = -Q(t) = -A\,\dot L_{\text{in}}(t). \]

Integrating in time from the reference instant $t_0$ to a general time $t$ and during the period where $\dot L_{\text{in}}=\dot L$, we get

\[ V_c(t) = V_c(t_0) - A\,[L(t)-L(t_0)]. \]

Since $V_c(t) = A_0 h_c(t)$, this implies

\begin{equation} h_c(t) = h_c(t_0) - \frac{A}{A_0}\,[L(t)-L(t_0)]. \label{eq:hc-general} \end{equation}

This form is completely general and does not assume any particular initial pattern of filled/empty segments. In particular, any portions of the pipe that are submerged in the container but initially empty (for example, all of $L_2$ and some part of $L_3$) simply modify the numerical values of $h_c(t_0)$ and $L(t_0)$, but do not change the linear relation

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}h_c}{\mathrm{d}L} = -\frac{A}{A_0}. \]

If we denote by $V_0$ the actual water volume in the container at $t=0$, and by $L_0 = L(0)$ the actual water length in the pipe at $t=0$, then $h_c(0) = V_0/A_0$ and eq. \eqref{eq:hc-general} can be written as

\begin{equation} h_c(t) = \frac{V_0 - A\,[L(t)-L_0]}{A_0}. \end{equation}

Here $V_0$ must exclude any submerged but initially empty pipe interior; those volumes are simply not water at $t=0$.

Lossless Equation of Motion: Filling Phase

We first derive the lossless equation of motion for the stage where the water plug is still advancing along the pipe, i.e.

\[ L(t_0) < L(t) < L_{\max}. \]

We model the water inside the pipe as a slug of length $L$, cross-sectional area $A$, density $\rho$, and mass

\[ m = \rho A L. \]

Let $g$ be the gravitational acceleration, and neglect friction and other losses. Atmospheric pressure acts both at the free surface in the container and at the pipe outlet, so it cancels in the driving pressure difference.

During the filling phase we have the kinematic relation

\begin{equation} v(t) = \dot L(t), \end{equation}

where $v(t)$ is the mean velocity in the pipe.

We will show below that the net driving force along the pipe is

\begin{equation} F_{\text{net}} = \rho g A \,\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr), \end{equation}

where $h(L)$ is the height of the outlet at the current front position. Applying Newton's second law $F = m\,\ddot L$ gives

\[ \rho A L \,\ddot L = \rho g A \,\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr), \]

so, after cancellation,

\[ L \,\ddot L = g \,\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr), \]

and hence the basic lossless differential equation during the filling phase is

\begin{equation} \ddot L = \frac{g}{L}\,\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr), \qquad L(t_0) < L(t) < L_{\max}, \label{eq:lossless-filling} \end{equation}

with $h(L)$ given by the piecewise definition above and $h_c(t)$ given by eq. \eqref{eq:hc-general}.

The filling phase ends at time $t_\ast$ such that $L(t_\ast) = L_{\max}$. Beyond this point, $L$ stays fixed and we must use a different set of equations (see Part II).

Detailed Derivation of the Net Driving Force

We now derive $F_{\text{net}}$ more explicitly by decomposing the contributions of pressure and gravity.

Local Force Balance on a Differential Element

Introduce a coordinate $s$ along the pipe, measured from the inlet (we will later identify $s$ with $L$ at the slug front). Consider a small fluid element of length $\mathrm{d}s$ and cross-sectional area $A$. Its volume is $A\,\mathrm{d}s$ and its mass is

\[ \mathrm{d}m = \rho A\,\mathrm{d}s. \]

Let $p(s,t)$ be the pressure and $h(s)$ the height of the centerline at position $s$. The tangent to the pipe makes a local angle $\theta(s)$ with the horizontal, so that

\[ \sin\theta(s) = \frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}s}. \]

The component of the weight along the pipe (positive in the direction of increasing $s$) is then

\[ \mathrm{d}F_g = -\rho g A \sin\theta(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = -\rho g A\,\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}s}\,\mathrm{d}s. \]

For this element, the forces in the tangential direction (taken as positive in the direction of increasing $s$) are:

  • Pressure on the upstream face (at $s$): $p(s,t)\,A$;
  • Pressure on the downstream face (at $s + \mathrm{d}s$): $-p(s+\mathrm{d}s,t)\,A$;
  • Component of weight along the pipe: \[ \mathrm{d}F_g = -\rho g A\,\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}s}\,\mathrm{d}s. \]

Thus the total tangential force on the element is

\[ \mathrm{d}F = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial s} A\,\mathrm{d}s - \rho g A \frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}s}\,\mathrm{d}s. \]

If the mean velocity in the slug is $v(t) = \dot L(t)$ and is uniform along the slug, then $\partial v/\partial s = 0$ and the local unsteady 1D momentum equation (Euler equation along a streamline) reduces to

\[ \rho \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial s} - \rho g \frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}s}. \]

Multiplying by $A\,\mathrm{d}s$ we recover

\[ \mathrm{d}F = \rho A\,\mathrm{d}s \,\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = \rho A\,\mathrm{d}s \,\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}, \]

which is Newton's second law for the differential element.

Integration Along the Entire Water Slug

Integrate along the slug from the entrance at $s=0$ (just inside the pipe) to the front of the slug at $s=L(t)$:

\begin{align} F_{\text{net}} &= \int_0^{L} \mathrm{d}F \\ &= \int_0^{L} \left( -\frac{\partial p}{\partial s} A - \rho g A\,\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}s} \right) \mathrm{d}s \\ &= -A\int_0^{L} \frac{\partial p}{\partial s} \,\mathrm{d}s -\rho g A\int_0^{L} \frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}s}\,\mathrm{d}s. \end{align}

The integrals can be evaluated explicitly:

  • Pressure contribution: \[ -A\int_0^{L} \frac{\partial p}{\partial s} \,\mathrm{d}s = -A\bigl(p(L,t) - p(0,t)\bigr) = A\bigl(p(0,t) - p(L,t)\bigr). \]
  • Gravity (body force) contribution: \[ -\rho g A\int_0^{L} \frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}s}\,\mathrm{d}s = -\rho g A \bigl(h(L) - h(0)\bigr) = \rho g A \bigl(h(0) - h(L)\bigr). \]

Therefore,

\begin{equation} F_{\text{net}} = A\bigl(p(0,t) - p(L,t)\bigr) + \rho g A \bigl(h(0) - h(L)\bigr). \label{eq:net-force-general} \end{equation}

Relating End Pressures to the Container and Outlet

We now connect $p(0,t)$ and $p(L,t)$ to the free-surface and outlet conditions.

  • At the container free surface ($z = h_c(t)$), the pressure is $p_{\text{atm}}$. Hydrostatic equilibrium in the container gives the pressure at the pipe inlet (which we call $p(0,t)$) as \[ p(0,t) = p_{\text{atm}} + \rho g \bigl(h_c(t) - h(0)\bigr), \] where $h(0)$ is the height of the pipe inlet.
  • At the outlet, we assume the jet discharges to the atmosphere at height $h(L)$, so the pressure just at the outlet inside the pipe is \[ p(L,t) = p_{\text{atm}}. \]

Substituting into \eqref{eq:net-force-general}:

\begin{align} F_{\text{net}} &= A\left[ p_{\text{atm}} + \rho g\bigl(h_c(t) - h(0)\bigr) - p_{\text{atm}} \right] + \rho g A \bigl(h(0) - h(L)\bigr) \\ &= \rho g A\bigl(h_c(t) - h(0)\bigr) + \rho g A\bigl(h(0) - h(L)\bigr) \\ &= \rho g A\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr). \end{align}

Interpretation

From the above decomposition we can interpret

\[ F_{\text{net}} = \underbrace{A\bigl(p(0,t) - p(L,t)\bigr)}_{\text{pressure drive}} + \underbrace{\rho g A \bigl(h(0) - h(L)\bigr)}_{\text{gravity drive}} = \rho g A\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr). \]

In other words:

  • The pressure difference between inlet and outlet, itself due to the water column in the container above the inlet, contributes a net push.
  • The body force (weight) of the water along the pipe projects along the pipe centerline and adds another contribution.
  • When these are combined, all dependence on the inlet height $h(0)$ cancels, and the net effect is equivalent to a single head difference between the free surface in the container at $h_c(t)$ and the outlet at $h(L)$.

Using $m = \rho A L$ and $F_{\text{net}} = m\,\ddot L$, we recover

\[ \rho A L \,\ddot L = \rho g A \bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr), \]

which is precisely the lossless equation \eqref{eq:lossless-filling}, now with the roles of pressure and gravity made explicit.

Part II: Loss Models and Phase-Split Dynamics

In this part we add distributed wall-friction losses (Darcy–Weisbach) and local (minor) losses, and we also distinguish explicitly between:

  1. The filling phase, where the front advances and $v = \dot L$;
  2. The draining phase, where the pipe is completely full and $L$ is fixed, but $v(t)$ and $h_c(t)$ still evolve.

We denote the pipe diameter by $D$, and we continue to use $v(t)$ for the mean velocity in the pipe.

Kinematics and Phases

Filling Phase ($L(t_0) < L(t) < L_{\max}$)

During the filling phase, the front of the water plug advances along the pipe, so

\begin{equation} v(t) = \dot L(t), \qquad L(t_0) < L(t) < L_{\max}. \end{equation}

The container height is given by eq. \eqref{eq:hc-general},

\[ h_c(t) = h_c(t_0) - \frac{A}{A_0}\,[L(t)-L(t_0)]. \]

The phase ends at time $t_\ast$ when $L(t_\ast) = L_{\max}$.

Draining Phase ($t \ge t_\ast$, full pipe)

Once the water plug occupies the entire pipe, its length becomes fixed:

\[ L(t) = L_{\max}, \qquad t \ge t_\ast, \]

and we must treat the mean velocity $v(t)$ as an independent state variable; in particular $v \neq \dot L$ since $\dot L = 0$.

The outlet height is now a constant

\[ h_{\text{out}} = h(L_{\max}). \]

The volumetric flow rate is $Q(t) = A v(t)$, so the container volume and height evolve according to

\begin{equation} \dot h_c(t) = -\,\frac{A}{A_0}\,v(t), \qquad t \ge t_\ast. \label{eq:hc-draining} \end{equation}

Distributed Friction: Darcy–Weisbach

The head loss due to wall friction over a length $L$ of pipe is

\begin{equation} h_f = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g}, \end{equation}

where

  • $f$ is the Darcy friction factor (dimensionless),
  • $L$ is the wetted pipe length,
  • $D$ is the pipe diameter,
  • $v$ is the mean velocity in the pipe.

The Reynolds number is

\[ \mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho v D}{\mu}, \]

with $\rho$ the density and $\mu$ the dynamic viscosity of the fluid.

For water at room temperature (approximately $\rho \approx 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$, $\mu \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$), typical regimes are:

  • Laminar flow ($\mathrm{Re} \lesssim 2000$): \[ f = \frac{64}{\mathrm{Re}}. \]
  • Turbulent flow: $f$ depends on both $\mathrm{Re}$ and the relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$. A common correlation is Colebrook–White: \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = -2\log_{10}\left( \frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{\mathrm{Re}\sqrt{f}} \right), \] where $\varepsilon$ is the roughness height.

Typical ranges of $f$ for water in pipes:

  • Smooth plastic tubing: $f \approx 0.015$–$0.02$ (high $\mathrm{Re}$);
  • Commercial steel pipe: $f \approx 0.02$–$0.03$.

Experimental Determination of $f$

In a straight pipe test section of known $L$ and $D$, the pressure drop $\Delta p$ is measured for a given flow rate (hence velocity $v$). The Darcy–Weisbach relation in pressure form is

\[ \Delta p = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{\rho v^2}{2}. \]

Solving for $f$ gives

\[ f = \frac{2D\,\Delta p}{\rho v^2 L}. \]

Repeating this at various $\mathrm{Re}$ and roughnesses leads to the classical Moody diagram and empirical correlations.

Local (Minor) Losses

Each fitting or geometric disturbance is modeled as

\begin{equation} h_m = K\,\frac{v^2}{2g}, \end{equation}

where $K$ is a dimensionless loss coefficient determined by geometry.

If several such elements are present along the flow path, the total minor-loss head is

\[ h_{\text{minor,tot}} = \left(\sum_i K_i\right)\frac{v^2}{2g}. \]

Typical values of $K$ (orders of magnitude) for water are:

  • Sharp-edged entrance from a reservoir: $K \approx 0.5$;
  • Well-rounded entrance: $K \approx 0.04$;
  • Sudden exit to a large tank / atmosphere: $K \approx 1.0$;
  • Smooth $90^\circ$ bend: $K \approx 0.2$–$0.5$;
  • Tight $90^\circ$ elbow: $K \approx 0.5$–$1.5$;
  • Fully open gate valve: $K \approx 0.1$–$0.2$;
  • Fully open globe valve: $K \approx 8$–$10$.

Experimental Determination of $K$

A test loop is set up with the fitting of interest. The extra pressure drop $\Delta p_{\text{extra}}$ across the fitting is measured, with straight-pipe losses subtracted out. Converting the extra drop to head,

\[ h_m = \frac{\Delta p_{\text{extra}}}{\rho g}, \]

the loss coefficient is

\[ K = \frac{2g\,h_m}{v^2}, \qquad v = \frac{Q}{A}, \]

where $Q$ is the volumetric flow rate and $A$ the pipe cross-sectional area.

Total Head Loss and Force

In the slug-flow model, over a wetted length $L$ we take the total head loss as

\begin{equation} h_{\text{loss}}(v,L) = f\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{v^2}{2g} + \left(\sum_i K_i\right)\frac{v^2}{2g}. \end{equation}

The corresponding resistive force (always opposing motion) is

\begin{equation} F_{\text{loss}} = \rho g A\,h_{\text{loss}}(v,L)\,\operatorname{sgn}(v), \end{equation}

where $\operatorname{sgn}(v)$ is the sign of the velocity. Since $v\,\operatorname{sgn}(v) = \lvert v\rvert$, we can equivalently write the combination $|v|v$.

Extended Equation of Motion: Filling Phase

The momentum balance with pressure/gravity driving and losses for the filling phase is

\[ \rho A L\,\ddot L = \rho g A\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr) - \rho g A\,h_{\text{loss}}(\dot L,L)\,\operatorname{sgn}(\dot L). \]

Substituting

\[ h_{\text{loss}}(\dot L,L) = \left( f\,\frac{L}{D} + \sum_i K_i \right)\frac{\dot L^2}{2g}, \]

and cancelling $\rho A$ gives

\[ L\,\ddot L = g\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr) - \left( f\,\frac{L}{D} + \sum_i K_i \right)\frac{|\dot L|\dot L}{2}. \]

Thus the extended filling-phase ODE is

\begin{equation} \ddot L = \frac{g}{L}\,\bigl(h_c(t) - h(L)\bigr) - \frac{1}{2L} \left( f\,\frac{L}{D} + \sum_i K_i \right)|\dot L|\dot L, \qquad L(t_0) < L(t) < L_{\max}. \label{eq:extended-filling} \end{equation}

Here:

  • $h(L)$ is the geometric height along the pipe,
  • $h_c(t)$ is given by eq. \eqref{eq:hc-general},
  • $f$ is the Darcy friction factor,
  • $K_i$ are the local loss coefficients.

When $f=0$ and all $K_i=0$, eq. \eqref{eq:extended-filling} reduces to the lossless form \eqref{eq:lossless-filling}.

Extended Equation of Motion: Draining Phase

For the draining phase ($t \ge t_\ast$) the water occupies the entire pipe, so $L(t) = L_{\max}$ is constant and the velocity $v(t)$ is the dynamical variable. The outlet height is $h_{\text{out}} = h(L_{\max})$.

The momentum balance for the full slug of length $L_{\max}$ is

\[ \rho A L_{\max}\,\dot v = \rho g A\bigl(h_c(t) - h_{\text{out}}\bigr) - \rho g A\,h_{\text{loss}}(v,L_{\max})\,\operatorname{sgn}(v), \]

with

\[ h_{\text{loss}}(v,L_{\max}) = \left( f\,\frac{L_{\max}}{D} + \sum_i K_i \right)\frac{v^2}{2g}. \]

Cancelling $\rho A$ gives

\[ L_{\max}\,\dot v = g\bigl(h_c(t) - h_{\text{out}}\bigr) - \left( f\,\frac{L_{\max}}{D} + \sum_i K_i \right)\frac{|v|v}{2}. \]

Together with eq. \eqref{eq:hc-draining}, the draining-phase dynamics are

\begin{align} \dot v &= \frac{g}{L_{\max}}\bigl(h_c(t) - h_{\text{out}}\bigr) - \frac{1}{2L_{\max}} \left( f\,\frac{L_{\max}}{D} + \sum_i K_i \right)|v|v, \label{eq:draining-v}\\[0.5em] \dot h_c(t) &= -\,\frac{A}{A_0}\,v(t), \label{eq:draining-hc} \end{align}

for $t \ge t_\ast$, where $t_\ast$ is the time at which $L(t_\ast)=L_{\max}$.

The initial conditions at the start of the draining phase are

\[ v(t_\ast) = \dot L(t_\ast), \qquad h_c(t_\ast) \text{ given by eq. \eqref{eq:hc-general} evaluated at } t_\ast. \]

In practice, one integrates the filling-phase ODE \eqref{eq:extended-filling} from $t_0$ until $L(t)=L_{\max}$, then switches to the draining system \eqref{eq:draining-system} with these initial values. The flow eventually ceases as $h_c(t)$ approaches $h_{\text{out}}$ and friction damps $v(t)$ to zero.

Summary of Variables and Parameters

  • State variables:
    • Filling phase: $L(t)$, $\dot L(t)$, $h_c(t)$.
    • Draining phase: $v(t)$, $h_c(t)$.
  • Geometry:
    • Node coordinates: $(0,h_0)$, $(x_1,h_1)$, …, $(x_4,h_4)$.
    • Segment lengths: $L_1,\dots,L_4$, total $L_{\max}$.
    • Height profile: $h(L)$ via segment maps $\tilde h_i$.
    • Outlet height: $h_{\text{out}} = h(L_{\max})$.
  • Areas and diameter:
    • Pipe area: $A$; container free-surface area: $A_0$; pipe diameter: $D$.
  • Loss parameters:
    • Darcy friction factor: $f$; minor-loss coefficients: $K_i$.
  • Fluid properties and constants:
    • Density: $\rho$; dynamic viscosity: $\mu$; gravity: $g$.
